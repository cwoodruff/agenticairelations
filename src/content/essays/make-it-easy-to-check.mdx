My essay on the Amdahl tax opened with "a finding that should stop
every DevRel leader cold," and then drew a conclusion the finding did
not support. I also misquoted Jeff Dean and got the arithmetic
backwards. The corrections are on that essay. The argument that
survived them is narrower, and I think more useful.

## What survived

Dean's actual point, made at NVIDIA GTC in March 2026 and
[reported by OfficeChai](https://officechai.com/ai/will-need-to-re-engineer-tools-so-that-they-can-keep-up-with-the-speeds-of-ai-agents-googles-jeff-dean/),
was about latency. Once an agent works far faster than a person, the
start-up time of its tools becomes "an Amdahl's Law-like bottleneck."

The arithmetic is simple. If 80% of the time an agent spends on an
integration goes to tools, sign-in, and waiting, then no model,
however fast, can make the whole more than 1.25 times faster. The gain
is in the 80%.

That much holds. But it describes one constraint, and I wrote as if it
were the only one.

## The one I missed

When the agent finishes, a person has to decide whether to trust what
it made.

A [Harvard study of engineering data, as reported by InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/10/jellyfish-ai-productivity/),
looked at what changed in companies that adopted coding agents. Lines
of code rose about 30%. Issues resolved rose about 12%. Time spent
reviewing pull requests rose 49%, and the share of pull requests that
needed changes doubled.

That comes from a news report, not the paper itself, and longer reviews do not
prove that review is what holds any particular team back. Lines of
code and resolved issues are also different things, so the gap between
them is not a measure of anything by itself. What the figures do is
point at something worth measuring: how long it takes a person to
check what an agent produced.

So there are two constraints on what an agent can do for your
developer. One is the time the agent spends on your platform. The
other is the time the developer spends making sure the agent got it
right.

## What a platform can do about the second

More than I first assumed. Whether a result is easy to verify is
largely a property of the platform it was built on.

**A place to try it safely.** A test mode or sandbox, so that checking
an integration does not mean risking real data or real money.

**Dry runs.** A way to ask what a call would do before it does it.

**Validators.** For configuration and for requests, so a mistake is
caught by a tool and not by a person reading a diff.

**A clear success signal.** "It worked" should be a fact the developer
can observe. If the only way to know is to read the code and reason
about it, the platform has left the hard part to the reviewer.

**Examples that check themselves.** A sample that ends with a test
teaches the agent, and the developer, what done looks like.

None of this is new. Good platforms have offered most of it for years,
for human developers. What has changed is the cost of not having it.
When a person wrote the integration, they understood it as they went.
When an agent wrote it, the understanding has to be rebuilt afterward,
and every one of these features makes that cheaper.

## Why this is DevRel's argument to make

Engineering builds sandboxes and validators. DevRel is where the
evidence for them turns up.

Run your evaluation and watch where a person has to step in. Count the
interventions. Note the tasks where the output looks plausible and is
wrong, because those are the ones that will pass a tired reviewer.
That list is a product roadmap argument, and nobody else in the
company is collecting it.

There is a measurement point here too. Strict grading changes the
picture.
[Stripe reports](https://stripe.dev/blog/stripe-integration-evals-methodology)
that when every necessary criterion has to pass, the average score in
its integration evaluations falls from 78.1% to 29.4%. Agents get
close far more often than they get there. Close is exactly what costs
a reviewer time.

## One exercise

Take a task your platform is known for. Have an agent do it. Then give
the result to a colleague who did not watch, and time how long it
takes them to be confident it is correct.

That number is not on any dashboard you have. It may be the one your
developers feel most.
